Cyberbullying Awareness - Story Books

Cyberbullying Story Book

The Invisible Ink (Target: Ages 6–9)

Leo loved his new tablet. He spent his afternoons playing "Pixel-Craft" and chatting with his school friends. One day, his friend Sam made a mistake in the game. Leo thought it would be funny to type, "Sam is a total noob. He ruins everything!" in the group chat. Other kids started typing "LOL" and "Yeah, Sam is the worst." Leo felt powerful until the next day at school. Sam sat alone at lunch, looking at his feet. When Leo said hello, Sam didn't look up. "I saw what you wrote," Sam whispered. "It made me feel like I shouldn't play anymore." Leo realized that his "funny" words felt like invisible ink that left real bruises. The Lesson: Before you hit send, imagine saying the words to the person's face. If it would hurt them in person, it will hurt them online. Leo deleted his comment and apologized in front of the whole group.

The Screenshot Savior (Target: Ages 10–13)

Maya was part of a dance team group chat. Lately, the messages had turned mean. Three girls were teasing Chloe about her practice outfit, calling her "Budget Ballerina." They sent edited photos of her looking silly. Maya felt sick to her stomach. She didn't want to be the next target, but she knew Chloe was crying at home. Instead of staying silent, Maya did three things: She Screenshot the mean messages. She Private Messaged Chloe to say, "I think they are being mean, and I’m on your side." She Showed her coach the screenshots. The coach sat the team down and explained that bullying isn't just physical—it's digital. Because Maya spoke up, the chat was deleted, and the girls had to sit out of the next performance. The Lesson: Being a "Bystander" helps the bully. Being an "Upstander" helps the victim. Always save evidence of cyberbullying to show a trusted adult.

The Block Button Power (Target: Ages 13–15)

Jake loved sharing his digital art on social media. But one week, a stranger started commenting on every post. "This is trash," the user wrote. "Give up, you have no talent." Soon, the stranger was sending direct messages telling Jake he shouldn't even bother going to art school. Jake stopped drawing. He kept checking his phone, waiting for the next mean comment. He felt like the bully was living inside his pocket. His older sister saw him moping. "Why are you giving a stranger the keys to your happiness?" she asked. She helped Jake use the Block and Report tools. Then, they set his account to "Private" so only friends could comment. Without the constant noise of the bully, Jake’s creativity came back. The Lesson: You are the boss of your social media. You have the right to block anyone who makes you feel unsafe or unhappy. Don't feed the trolls—block them.

The Group Chat Ripple (Target: Teens/Young Adults)

A rumor started with a single "Send." A photo of Sarah, taken out of context at a party, was circulating with a fake caption. By the end of the hour, it had been shared fifty times. Sarah felt like the whole world was laughing at a lie. Her classmate, Ethan, saw the photo pop up in his feed. He was about to forward it to his best friend when he stopped. He thought about how quickly a "share" can ruin a life. Ethan didn't just ignore it; he replied to the thread: "This isn't cool, guys. It’s a fake story. Delete it." A few others, seeing Ethan's courage, liked his comment and stopped sharing the photo. The "ripple" of the rumor hit a wall and eventually faded away. The Lesson: Cyberbullying spreads because people forward it. You can stop the chain. If you receive a post intended to embarrass someone, delete it. Your choice to not share is a choice to protect someone.

The Photo Without Permission (Target: Ages 9–12)

Harriet and Mia were best friends who loved making dance videos. One afternoon, they filmed a new routine in Harriet’s backyard. In one part of the video, Mia tripped over a garden hose and landed in a pile of leaves, making a very funny face. Without asking, Harriet posted the "blooper" clip to her story, thinking it was hilarious. By the time Mia saw it, the video had hundreds of views, and classmates were already making stickers of her "leaf face" to use as memes. Mia didn't think it was funny; she felt embarrassed and betrayed. When Mia asked her to take it down, Harriet realized that even though they were friends, she didn't have the right to share Mia’s image without her "OK." The Lesson: Consent is key. Just because a photo is on your phone doesn't mean it should be on everyone’s screen. Always ask for permission before posting a picture or video of someone else.

The Ghost of Posts Past (Target: Older Teens/Young Adults)

Ryan was a high school senior with a bright future. He was a great student and a star athlete. Four years ago, when he was just starting middle school, he thought it was "cool" to post edgy, mean-spirited jokes on his public social media profile to get more likes. He eventually grew up and stopped, but he never deleted the old posts. When Ryan applied for a prestigious university scholarship, he was a finalist. However, during the background check, the committee found those old comments. Even though Ryan wasn't that person anymore, the "digital footprint" he left behind made them question his character. Ryan had to work twice as hard to prove he had changed, and he learned that the internet is like permanent ink. The Lesson: Your digital reputation is a byproduct of everything you do online, and it follows you into adulthood. Use the "Pause before Send" rule: if you wouldn't want a future employer or coach to see it, don't post it. It is much easier to think twice now than to try and "clean up" a digital footprint later.
